When nice meant foolish

Seven hundred years ago, calling someone "nice" was an insult.

Time ~2 min Win a word story that surprises native speakers Bonus 1 C1 word

Guess firstWhen "nice" first appeared in English, around 1300, what did it mean? Decide before you scroll.

"Nice" comes from Latin nescius, "not knowing", and first meant foolish or ignorant.

Why it feels wrong: today "nice" is the mildest compliment there is. But nescius is ne- ("not") + scire ("to know"), and Old French nice meant simple or stupid.1 In medieval English it was used to call people silly.2 Over centuries it drifted through "timid", "fussy" and "precise" (still heard in "a nice distinction") and reached "pleasant" only in the 1700s.1

Retell it · say it out loud once
  1. Hook: "Would you be offended if someone called you nice? In 1300 you should have been."
  2. Detail: "It comes from Latin nescius, 'not knowing'. It meant foolish, then fussy, then precise, and only became 'pleasant' in the 1700s."
  3. Kicker: "'Nice' took nearly 500 years to travel from 'foolish' to 'pleasant'."

pejorativeadjective

Expressing disapproval, or suggesting that something is not good or not important.3 In medieval English, "nice" was a pejorative word: it meant foolish.
