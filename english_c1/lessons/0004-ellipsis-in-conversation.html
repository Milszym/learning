Lesson 4 · Grammar

Ellipsis in conversation

How "Seen it.", "Neither have I." and "I'd love to." say a whole sentence in two words.

Time ~12 min Win fill in the missing words of 4 kinds of short reply Mission follow fast, clipped dialogue without losing the thread

The big idea

In conversation, natives leave out anything the listener can recover from context. This is ellipsis, and in informal speech it's completely normal; nobody hears it as "missing" words.1 A related trick is substitution: a small word like so, not or an auxiliary stands in for a whole clause.1 To follow fast dialogue, you need to rebuild the full sentence in your head, instantly.

Polish trap

Ja też / Ja też nie can't be translated word for word: English repeats the auxiliary from the first sentence (So do I, Neither have I). And Mam nadzieję, że nie is I hope not, never I don't hope so. Myślę, że nie is usually I don't think so.

The four patterns

So do I / Neither have I

so / neither / nor + auxiliary + subject

"I'm knackered." "So am I."

"I haven't seen it." "Neither have I." (or I haven't either)23

No auxiliary in the first line? Use do / does / did: "She loved it." "So did I."

I hope so / I'm afraid not

so = yes clause · not = negative clause

"Will it stay dry?" "I hope so." / "I hope not."

"Is he in?" "I'm afraid not." (polite bad news)

With think, believe, expect: I don't think so. I think not sounds formal or old-fashioned.4

I'd love to

keep "to", drop the rest

"Fancy a drink later?" "I'd love to." (= I'd love to have a drink)

"Why don't you just move?" "We don't want to."15

Sounds good. Want a lift?

drop the subject and/or auxiliary

[It] Sounds good. · [Do you] Want a lift?

[I've] Seen it. · [Are] You coming?15

Register. Dropping subjects and auxiliaries is informal, and mostly happens at the start of a sentence. People don't usually drop the auxiliary with I in questions: Am I too loud?, not I too loud?1 Me too and Me neither are common, very casual alternatives to So do I and Neither do I.
