Lesson 11 · Listening

Whatcha gonna do?

Decode the squashed words of fast dialogue: gonna, dunno, didja, innit and friends.

Time ~12 min Win turn 14 reduced forms back into full English instantly Mission follow fast, casual dialogue without rewinding

The big idea

In fast speech, words blur together: "the pronunciation of a word will change depending on the words around it."1 You already know one result: shoulda = should have. Your job here is only to hear these forms and rebuild the full words, not to write them.

Spelling warning

Spellings like gonna or dunno are informal and non-standard. You'll see them in subtitles, song lyrics and text messages. Oxford's advice: don't write them "unless you are copying somebody's speech."2

Why it happens

Weak forms (schwa)

unstressed to, of, a, you → /ə/

a piece of cake → /ə piːs ə keɪk/3

That's where gonna, wanna, kinda, outta get their final -a.

Elision (dropped sounds)

/t/ or /d/ between consonants disappears

I can't do it → "I can' do it"4

next week → "nex' week"

Assimilation (sounds merge)

/d/ + you → /dʒ/ · /t/ + you → /tʃ/

would you → "wou-ja"; did you → "didja"5

meet you → "mee-chu"; what (are) you → "whatcha"6

Trap: wotcha

In British English, Wotcha! on its own is a friendly hello (originally from what cheer?), not "what are you".11 Wotcha, Dave! = Hi, Dave!

Real-world task · next episode you watch

Watch 10 minutes with English subtitles off. Each time you hear a squashed chunk (gonna, didja, whatcha, innit), pause and say the full form out loud. Then rewatch the same 10 minutes with subtitles on and check. With Language Reactor, search the subtitle panel for "gonna" or "wanna". Bring the hardest line back to the chat.

Primary source BBC Learning English: Tim's Pronunciation Workshop. Short videos with real Londoners, each one on a single process (schwa, elision of /t/, Would you…?). Watch one a day; ear training works best in small doses.
